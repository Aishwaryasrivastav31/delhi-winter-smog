Abstract

Every October and November, Delhi's air turns from bad to dangerous, and three suspects are named every year: crop-residue fires in Punjab and Haryana, festival fireworks and the weather. This study measures each over eight burning seasons (2018–2025), then asks whether acting about a month earlier would help.

Fire smoke is identified from a natural experiment: on any day, the wind decides whether the same fires blow towards Delhi or away from it. Comparing days within the same week, crop fires cause 14.7% of Delhi's October–November PM2.5 (95% CI 7.4%–22.0%), while the same fires downwind explain nothing (-0.2%). Shuffling the wind 1,000 times reproduces a share this large only 2% of the time.

Satellite fire detections fell 90% between 2021 and 2025, yet the smoke's share did not: one detection now stands for about 8× more PM2.5, consistent with burning moved away from the satellite's afternoon pass. Diwali night runs at 2.3× an ordinary night in all 8 years, but adds about 10% to its week and 3.4% to its month. Dussehra is not visible.

Enforcing GRAP from 1 September would lower season PM2.5 by at most 3.4%–3.7%, and by nothing at Study 1's estimate. Real October cuts do more: a 30% cut in Delhi's own emissions lowers October PM2.5 by about 11%, and halving October fires by about 6.5%.

02Data

  • Air quality: CPCB CAAQMS hourly data, 2018–2025, Anand Vihar, Ashok Vihar, Aya Nagar and Bawana (280,512 hourly rows; 10,571 valid station-days, at least 18 of 24 hours).
  • Fires: NASA FIRMS VIIRS active fires, vegetation fires only, Punjab–Haryana, more than 40 km from Delhi: 742,861 S-NPP detections (2018–2025) and 115,271 NOAA-20 detections (2024–2025, a check).

Integrity check. The CPCB repository served one station's data under other station names. The pipeline now stops if any two stations match in more than half of their hours; the largest match in the final data is 1.4%.

03Design: let the wind decide

Each fire is weighted by how directly that day's wind blows from it towards Delhi. The same fires, weighted the opposite way, form a downwind placebo: if smoke is the cause, only the upwind version should matter.

FireUpd = Σfires max(0, cos(windd − bearingfire)) / 1000

Station-day PM2.5 (October–November, 485 days, 1,822 station-days) is regressed on upwind and downwind fires (same day, 1 and 2 days before) with one fixed effect for each calendar week of each year, 8 wind sectors, temperature, humidity, rain, wind speed, weekday, station, and Diwali and Dussehra days. Standard errors are Driscoll–Kraay. Because one satellite detection does not stand for the same burning in every year (section 5), the effect is estimated separately for 2018–2023 and 2024–2025.

05Fewer detections, the same smoke

October–November fire detections fell from 96,664 in 2021 to 10,033 in 2025, a 90% drop. Delhi's PM2.5 did not fall, and neither did the smoke's share. What changed is what one detection means: the effect per 1,000 detections is 241 in 2024–25 against 32 in 2018–23 (difference p = 0.014).

The detections themselves also changed: the night share rose from 0.3–0.9% to about 4%, and median fire power fell from 4.5–5.1 to 3.8 MW. That is consistent with burning moved away from the satellite's 13:30 pass. A model with one effect for all years would put the 2024–25 share at 2%; that is an undercount.

The heaviest smoke days carry the effect

PM2.5 vs days with no upwind fire, by fifths of same-day upwind exposure within each year. Only the heaviest fifth is significant.

What did not go cleanly, and is reported as it came out.

  • Future fires in 2024–25. Fires 1–2 days ahead predict today's PM2.5 in 2024–25 (+106, p < 0.001) but not in 2018–23 (+11, p = 0.22). This fits the same timing shift: burning missed by today's pass is detected tomorrow. The share stays at 14.8% with them added.
  • Carbon monoxide does not rise with upwind fires (+0.06 mg/m³, p = 0.11); Delhi's CO is dominated by traffic.
  • Wheat season. April–May fires add 4.6% (CI 1.8%–7.3%).

07Festivals

Diwali night versus an ordinary night

Diwali

2.3× on the night, in all 8 years

20:00–02:00 averages 500 µg/m³ against 222 on ordinary nights (sign test p = 0.008); the peak hour reached 7.0× in 2025. Diwali accounts for 10% of the following week, 3.4% of its month and 0.5%–1.7% of the season. In calm years (wind < 2.5 m/s) it was 5.7% of its month; in windy years about 0%.

Dussehra

No measurable effect

The night is 1.2× an ordinary night (above baseline in 6 of 8 years, p = 0.29), and the two-day regression effect is -7 µg/m³ (p = 0.66).

09Would acting a month earlier help?

Severe days come after 1 November; unsafe air starts in October

Of 106 severe days (above 250 µg/m³) in Oct–Nov 2018–2025, 10 came before 1 November. October averaged 1.3–2.5× India's 24-hour standard every year.

10Conclusion

Crop-fire smoke causes about 15% of Delhi's October–November PM2.5, a share that held steady even as satellite fire counts fell by 90%, which means falling fire counts are not proof of less burning. Diwali is a sharp one-night spike, Dussehra is not visible, and weather decides how much pollution stays over the city. Starting the emergency plan a month earlier would change little; real cuts in October, at home and in the fields, are what would move the number.
